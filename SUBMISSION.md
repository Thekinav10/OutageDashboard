# HACKONEX Finals — Problem Statement

## The Outage Nobody Noticed for Six Hours

### Background

In October 2021, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp went offline globally for
about six hours. The root cause wasn't a hack or a hardware failure — it was
a routine configuration change to the company's backbone network that had an
error in it. That single bad change caused the company's own systems to
withdraw the routes that told the rest of the internet how to reach
Facebook's servers. Other services that depended on those same internal
systems (including the tools engineers needed to log in and fix the
problem) went down too, making the outage worse and harder to repair. The
warning signs weren't a sudden crash — they were a slow, cascading
unraveling that outside observers only recognized once everything was
already down.

### Scenario

You are the on-call monitoring team for a mid-sized company running a
microservices-based platform — an application made of many small,
independent backend services (login, payments, search, notifications, etc.)
that all talk to each other over the network. An engineer pushes a routine
configuration change to the network layer that connects these services. The
change looks harmless in the deployment log and passes automated checks.
But it introduces a subtle routing error.

Instead of anything crashing immediately, the effect ripples outward
slowly: one service starts taking slightly longer to respond, which causes
services that depend on it to queue up requests, which causes *their*
dependents to slow down too — until, roughly six hours later, the whole
platform is unusable. At no point does anything log a hard "ERROR" or
"DOWN" — everything just gets progressively, deceptively slower, mixed in
with the platform's normal, everyday noise (traffic spikes, minor blips,
scheduled jobs).

### What you're given

- **`config_changes.csv`** — a timestamped record of every configuration
  change made to any service during the window, including the one bad
  change (not flagged as bad).
- **`dependency_map.json`** — which services call which other services.
- **`metrics_stream.csv`** — latency, request volume, and error rate per
  service, sampled at regular intervals across the window — mostly normal,
  noisy fluctuation, with the real degradation buried inside it.

### Your task

1. Ingest the metrics and config-change data.
2. Detect the early, subtle signs of the cascading failure — rising latency
   correlated with the dependency chain — well before any service fully
   fails, and distinguish this from ordinary noisy fluctuations that don't
   lead anywhere.
3. Trace the degradation back through the dependency map to identify the
   *originating* config change as the likely root cause — not just the
   service that's currently the most broken, which will usually be a
   downstream symptom, not the cause.
4. Produce a clear, human-readable incident report: what started it, how it
   spread, and when a human should have intervened — before full outage.

### Constraints that make this hard

- No entry in the data is explicitly labeled "this is the bad one" — you
  have to infer it from patterns.
- The platform's normal operation already contains noise (real spikes, real
  blips) that must **not** trigger false alarms — a system that cries wolf
  on ordinary traffic is a failed solution, even if it happens to also
  catch the real event.
- The failure spreads through *dependency relationships*, not just
  "everything gets slow at once" — a good solution has to reason about the
  service graph, not just look at each service's metrics in isolation.

### Deliverables

- A working dashboard or API showing detection of the cascading failure
  (live or replayed against the provided data).
- A short written incident report generated by your system: root cause,
  propagation path, and the earliest point at which the failure was
  detectable.

### Evaluation criteria

- Correctly identifies the root-cause config change — not just a
  downstream symptom.
- Detects it early — more of the window "spared" scores higher.
- False-positive rate on the normal noise already present in the data.
- Clarity and usability of the dashboard/report.
